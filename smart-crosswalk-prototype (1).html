System parts (turn on or off)

The stages you would go through to build the real smart crosswalk. Each one lists the materials, plus the math and science it uses.

What happens

  • Measure the road and crosswalk, count lanes, and note where poles and sensors will go.
  • Check collision data to see when the crosswalk is busiest (in Alberta, 26.9% of serious pedestrian collisions happen 3 to 7 pm).

Materials

  • Measuring wheel or tape, graph paper or CAD software, traffic counter

Math

  • Measurement and scale drawings (1 square = 1 m)
  • Area: 15 m × 4 m = 60 m² of crosswalk
  • Reading percentages from collision data

Science

  • Collecting and analyzing data before designing

What happens

  • Work out how much energy the crosswalk uses, then size the solar panel and battery so it runs all night, even in winter.

Materials

  • Solar panel (about 300 W), charge controller, 12 V lithium iron phosphate battery, copper wiring, weatherproof electrical box

Math

  • Energy = power × time (W × h = Wh)
  • Comparing energy in vs energy out

Science

  • Energy transformations (light → electrical → chemical)
  • Electric circuits
  • Photovoltaic effect in solar cells

What happens

  • Cut channels into the road, lay conduit back to the pole, set LED strips in aluminum channels on top of pressure sensors, and seal them under a clear, textured cover.

Materials

  • Tempered glass or polycarbonate covers, aluminum channels, waterproof (IP68) LED strips, pressure (load) sensors, concrete, gravel, anti-slip grit, electrical conduit

Math

  • Total LED length: 12 × 3.6 m + 2 × 15 m ≈ 73 m
  • Measuring depth so tiles sit flush with the road

Science

  • Light: LEDs turn electrical energy into light
  • Friction on a wet or icy surface
  • Material strength and freeze/thaw (water expands when it freezes)

What happens

  • Wire every crosswalk tile's pressure sensor back to the pole, add pressure mats at each curb, and mount light and weather sensors on top of the pole.
  • The tiles tell the system where the walker is, so no camera or radar is needed.

Materials

  • 25 pressure-sensing LED tiles, pressure mats, signal wire in conduit, light sensor, temperature and precipitation sensor, amber LED beacons, steel pole (about 3 m)

Math

  • Position from tile number: tile × 0.6 m
  • Speed = distance ÷ time between footsteps
  • 15 m ÷ 0.6 m = 25 tiles

Science

  • Pressure = force ÷ area
  • Weight = mass × gravity (9.8 m/s²)
  • Sensors turn a push (force) into an electrical signal

What happens

  • Build a tough, weatherproof panel with language buttons, two crossing-time buttons, a screen, a speaker, and tactile features.

Materials

  • Vandal-proof aluminum housing, large push buttons, small outdoor LCD screen, weatherproof speaker, raised tactile arrow, braille plate

Math

  • Time = distance ÷ speed: 12.5 s vs 18.75 s
  • Percent change: 18.75 ÷ 12.5 = 1.5, so 50% more time

Science

  • Sound waves: the speaker gets louder when traffic is noisy
  • Designing for accessibility (vision, mobility, language)

What happens

  • Write the program that reads the buttons and sensors, stops traffic, and keeps checking whether the walker has enough time.

Materials

  • Microcontroller (for example an Arduino or Raspberry Pi), traffic signal controller connection, code

Math

  • Inequalities: is time needed > time left?
  • Time = distance ÷ speed, recalculated 10 times a second

Science

  • Computer science: algorithms, loops and if/then decisions

Stage 7: Test it

Before building for real, the system is tested in the Simulation. Tests to run:

  • A slow walker presses Standard. Does the system add time?
  • A walker stops in the middle. Does the countdown hold?
  • Night mode: are the walker and crosswalk easy to see?
  • Snow: does it plan for slower walking and turn the LEDs up?
  • Each language: does the message change and get spoken?
  • Turn parts off one at a time (or all off for a normal crosswalk) to see what each part adds.

What happens

  • Test with a model, fix problems, then pilot one real crosswalk before building more.

Math

  • Compare results: crossings completed, how often time was added, how often the signal ran out mid-crossing

Science

  • Fair testing: change one condition at a time
